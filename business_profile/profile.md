## THE PROBLEM I SOLVE

**The Foundational Operations Gap.**

90% of B2B tech companies skip straight to fancy automation without building the operational foundation first. They end up with a Formula 1 car on a dirt road.

The symptoms show up everywhere:
- Lead scoring that ranks test users above real CEOs
- Marketing says they sent 200 MQLs; sales says they got 50 (nobody's lying — the terms were never defined)

Companies treat the symptoms (patch this workflow, fix that report) instead of curing the disease (build the infrastructure that makes everything else work).

## HOW I SOLVE IT

**The Tower of Power** — five foundational layers that build on each other, bottom-up. Get them right and your automation actually works. Skip one and everything above it collapses.

### Layer 1: Database Health
Your database is the literal foundation. Clean data, proper field architecture, deduplication, data governance, minimum viable record standards. You can't score leads when "CEO" and "Chief Executive Officer" are different values.

### Layer 2: Segmentation
Meaningful audience segments based on clean data. Explicit, filterable definitions — not loose job title filtering and good intentions. Your "Enterprise" segment shouldn't include a 10-person startup and a 50,000-employee company.

### Layer 3: Customer Lifecycle
Defined stages, proper transition criteria, written definitions that marketing AND sales actually agree on. When everyone defines "qualified" differently, your pipeline reports are fiction.

### Layer 4: Prioritization
Lead scoring that weights fit over engagement. Score who they ARE before you score what they DO. Without this, your best prospects sit in the cold lead pile while test users get the red carpet.

### Layer 5: Speed to Lead
NOW automation works. When a qualified lead raises their hand, your system responds in minutes, not days. But only after Layers 1-4 are solid — otherwise you're just speeding garbage to sales reps who already don't trust marketing.

**The order matters. Skip a layer, everything above it collapses.**

## MY UNIQUE POINT OF VIEW

### 1. You're automating chaos.
Most companies buy a platform, hire someone to "figure it out," and forget to document anything. When that person leaves, the next hire is too scared to touch anything. So they build a parallel system. Now you have two sources of truth and nobody trusts either.

### 2. People and process before platform.
The industry defaults to "platform-first" thinking. New CMO arrives, rips out the old stack, buys a new one, and discovers the same problems follow them. Because the problem was never the platform. It was the data, the definitions, and the processes underneath.

### 3. Fix what's broken before you build what's new.
Everyone wants to start at Layer 5 (speed to lead, automation, instant routing). But without Layers 1-4, you're just building a more efficient garbage machine. You're notifying sales reps about leads that aren't qualified and triggering sequences for people who don't match your ICP.

### 4. The unsexy work is the highest-ROI work.
Nobody gets promoted for cleaning a database. Nobody gets a LinkedIn standing ovation for documenting lifecycle stages. But it's the work that determines whether your next campaign, hire, or platform investment actually produces results.

### 5. Best practices are bullshit when your foundations are broken.
Every HubSpot tutorial, every Marketo playbook, every "marketing ops playbook" on LinkedIn assumes you already have clean data, standardized fields, and agreed-upon definitions. Your actual reality is 58 custom fields (your team uses 3), five different spellings of "organic," and an MQL definition that nobody agreed on.

### 6. Surgery on a patient that's awake.
I'm not building systems in a vacuum. These are live businesses with real revenue at stake. The fixes have to work without breaking what's already running. That's what makes this hard — and that's what makes experience matter.

### 7. 51% therapist, 49% consultant.
Half of this job is navigating the emotional reality of broken systems — the blame, the fear of change, the institutional resistance, the politics between marketing and sales. The technical work is the easy part. Getting humans aligned is where the real work happens.
